1 · Two ways to represent the world

Vector and raster

Vector

Discrete objects with coordinates.

  • Point — a fix, a nest, a trap
  • Line — a trajectory, a river, a road
  • Polygon — a home range, a park boundary, a lake

Each object carries an attribute table.

Exact geometry, arbitrary precision.

Raster

A regular grid of cells, each with a value.

  • Elevation, NDVI, temperature, land cover
  • Defined by origin, cell size, number of rows/cols, CRS

Continuous surfaces, fixed resolution.

Fast maths, no notion of individual objects.

Movement analysis is nearly always points (or steps) sampling rasters. That one sentence describes Sessions 3 and 7.

The same place, both ways

Cilla’s fixes, one week of her track and her range as vectors; the terrain under them as a raster.

Choosing the model

Question Model
Where exactly was the animal at 08:00? Vector (point)
What is the NDVI there? Raster, sampled at that point
What is the park boundary? Vector (polygon)
How far is the nearest river? Raster (a distance surface) or vector + distance computation
How much forest within 500 m? Raster, summarised in a buffer

The same reality can be either. A river is a line if you care about its course, and a distance-to-river raster if you care about how far the animal is from it. Choose by the operation you need, not by what the thing “is”.

2 · Coordinate reference systems

The problem

The Earth is a lumpy ellipsoid. Maps and computer screens are flat. Getting from one to the other requires choices, and every choice distorts something.

A CRS answers three questions:

  1. What shape is the Earth? — the ellipsoid (e.g. WGS84, GRS80)
  2. How is that shape tied to the actual planet? — the datum
  3. How do we flatten it? — the projection (or none, if we keep angles)

Geographic coordinates

Longitude and latitude are angles, not distances

Latitude: angle north/south of the equator. Longitude: angle east/west of Greenwich. Units are degrees. This is what every GPS and every Movebank file gives you — usually WGS84, EPSG:4326.

Why you cannot do arithmetic with degrees

  • One degree of latitude ≈ 111 km everywhere
  • One degree of longitude ≈ 111 km × cos(latitude)
    • at the equator: 111 km
    • at 24° (Kruger): ~102 km
    • at 46° (Switzerland): ~77 km
    • at the pole: 0 km
# WRONG — mixes two different units
sqrt((x2 - x1)^2 + (y2 - y1)^2)

Euclidean distance on longitude/latitude is meaningless. You either project to a metric CRS, or compute great-circle distances on the ellipsoid. sf does the second automatically for EPSG:4326 — which is safe, but slower, and does not fix your plots.

One degree is not one distance

Every circle has an 800 km radius on the ground. Plotted as if degrees were metres, they stretch east–west — badly at high latitudes.

Projected coordinates

Every projection preserves some properties and destroys others

Conformal keeps angles/shape (Mercator, UTM). Equal-area keeps area (Albers, Mollweide). Equidistant keeps distances from a point. No projection keeps everything.

UTM: the practical default

  • The world is cut into 60 zones, each 6° of longitude wide
  • Each zone gets its own transverse Mercator projection, so distortion inside a zone is small (< 0.1%)
  • Coordinates in metres: easting and northing
  • Kruger is in zone 36S → EPSG:32736
  • Switzerland is in zone 32N → EPSG:32632

UTM works beautifully inside one zone. Leo the vulture crosses many zones and 47° of latitude, so no single UTM zone fits him. For continental-scale tracks use an equal-area CRS suited to the region — or work in geographic coordinates with geodesic distances.

Choose the projection for the analysis

No projection preserves everything, so pick the property your analysis needs:

You will compute… Preserve… Typical choice
Step lengths, distances distance local UTM zone, or azimuthal equidistant centred on the study area
Home-range areas, densities area Albers or Lambert equal-area (LAEA)
Directions, turning angles angles / shape conformal: UTM (locally)
A continent-scale migration area, usually LAEA centred on the flyway

Inside one UTM zone the distortions are under 0.1%, so UTM does all three well enough. The choice only becomes real when your animal leaves the zone.

EPSG codes — the vocabulary you need

EPSG What Units
4326 WGS84 geographic — GPS, Movebank degrees
3857 Web Mercator — Google/OSM basemaps. Never for analysis metres
32736 UTM zone 36S — Kruger metres
32632 UTM zone 32N — Switzerland, Italy metres
3035 ETRS89 / LAEA Europe — equal-area, continental metres
6933 WGS84 / NSIDC EASE-Grid 2.0 Global — equal-area, global metres

3857 is a trap. It is conformal, wildly non-equal-area, and areas near the poles are inflated by orders of magnitude. Use it to display tiles, never to compute a home range.

The same circles, two projections

Every red disc covers the same 2.5 million km². Web Mercator inflates them towards the poles; an equal-area projection keeps their areas equal, at the cost of their shapes.

Quiz

You have buffalo fixes in EPSG:4326 and you want the area of a 95% home range polygon, in km². What is the right sequence?

Project first, to a CRS whose units are metres and which is appropriate for the region — UTM 36S for Kruger. Then all the geometry operations are ordinary plane geometry and areas come out in m².

There is no fixed conversion from degrees² to km²: the factor depends on latitude. Web Mercator (3857) has units of metres but is not equal-area, so areas are systematically wrong. And while sf can compute geodesic lengths and areas on 4326, kernel-density home-range estimators need projected coordinates — they assume a plane.

3 · A short, messy history

Why your old tutorial no longer runs

Era Vector Raster Geometry engine
2005–2023 sp raster rgdal, rgeos
2016 → sf sf (GDAL, GEOS, PROJ built in)
2020 → terra terra
  • rgdal, rgeos and maptools were retired from CRAN in October 2023; raster now runs on top of terra
  • Most Stack Overflow answers and many published scripts still use sp/raster
  • Some movement packages — adehabitatLT, adehabitatHR — still expect sp objects. You will convert with as(x, "Spatial") or sf::as_Spatial()

Write new code in sf + terra. Recognise sp + raster when you read it.

4 · sf — vector data

The idea behind sf

A simple feature data frame is an ordinary data frame with one extra column, geometry, that holds the coordinates — and a CRS attached.

```{r}
library(sf)

pts <- data.frame(
  id = c("a", "b"),
  lon = c(31.76, 31.80),
  lat = c(-24.54, -24.50)
)

pts_sf <- st_as_sf(pts, coords = c("lon", "lat"), crs = 4326, remove = FALSE)
pts_sf
```

Dimension:     XY

1  a 31.76 -24.54 POINT (31.76 -24.54)

Everything else is dplyr

Because it is a data frame, all your usual verbs work, and the geometry comes along for the ride.

```{r}
library(dplyr)

pts_sf |>
  mutate(label = toupper(id)) |>
  filter(lat > -24.52) |>
  select(id, label)
```

Note the geometry column survives select() without being named — it is “sticky”. Use st_drop_geometry() when you genuinely want a plain data frame.

st_crs() vs st_transform() — the classic bug

st_crs(x) <- 32736

“These numbers were always UTM; I am just writing down the label.”

If the numbers are actually degrees, you have now lied to R. Your buffalo are now in Antarctica (next slide).

st_transform(x, 32736)

“These numbers are in the current CRS; compute the corresponding UTM coordinates.”

The numbers change. This is almost always what you want.

Assign a CRS only when the data arrived without one and you know what it should be. Otherwise, transform.

Where the mislabelled buffalo went

(31.76, −24.54) read as metres in UTM 36S is a spot 31 m east and 24 m south of the zone’s false origin: 500 km west of the 33°E meridian and 10,000 km south of the equator.

That is (57°W, 85.5°S) — the Antarctic ice sheet. No error, no warning.

Quiz

Your colleague sends a CSV of coordinates like x = 375051, y = 7285726 and says “they’re from Kruger”. What are they, and what do you do?

Magnitudes give it away. Longitude and latitude are bounded by ±180 and ±90, so six- and seven-digit values cannot be degrees. Values of that size in metres with a large northing are classic UTM, and Kruger is zone 36S (EPSG:32736) — the 7,285,726 m northing is the southern-hemisphere false northing at ~24.5°S.

Because the numbers are already UTM, you declare the CRS when building the sf object (crs = 32736) rather than transforming. Transforming would treat metres as degrees.

Sanity check afterwards: st_transform(x, 4326) and confirm you land at roughly 31.8°E, 24.5°S.

5 · terra — raster data

A raster is a grid plus metadata

```{r}
library(terra)

r <- rast(nrows = 4, ncols = 6,
          xmin = 31.5, xmax = 32.1, ymin = -24.8, ymax = -24.4,
          crs = "EPSG:4326")
values(r) <- 1:ncell(r)
r
```
class       : SpatRaster
size        : 4, 6, 1  (nrow, ncol, nlyr)
resolution  : 0.1, 0.1  (x, y)
extent      : 31.5, 32.1, -24.8, -24.4  (xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax)
coord. ref. : lon/lat WGS 84 (EPSG:4326)
source(s)   : memory
name        : lyr.1
min value   :     1
max value   :    24

What defines a raster

  • Extent — xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax
  • Resolution — cell size in CRS units. res(r)
  • Dimensions — rows × columns × layers. dim(r)
  • CRS — same concept as for vectors
  • Values — one per cell per layer, possibly NA

A multi-layer raster (SpatRaster with several layers) is how you store a time series: one NDVI layer per 16-day composite, stacked. That is exactly what we need in Session 3.

The terra functions you will use

Function What it does
rast() create or read a raster
crop() / mask() cut to an extent / to a polygon
project() reproject (note: not st_transform)
extract() sample raster values at points — the key operation
aggregate() / resample() change resolution
terrain() slope, aspect, from elevation
distance() distance-to-feature surface
global(), zonal() summaries
writeRaster() save, usually GeoTIFF

extract() is the bridge between the movement world and the environment world. Almost everything from Session 3 onwards passes through it.

Rasters and vectors must agree

Before extract(), three things must line up:

  1. Same CRS. If both objects carry a CRS, terra transforms for you and warns. If either lacks one — a bare coordinate matrix — it assumes they match.
  2. Overlapping extents. Non-overlap gives you a column of NA, not an error.
  3. Appropriate resolution. Extracting a 1 km NDVI cell for a fix with 10 m GPS error is fine. Extracting a 10 m Sentinel pixel for an Argos fix with 5 km error is self-deception.

Match the resolution of your environmental data to the error of your locations. A pixel finer than your positional error adds noise, not information.

Quiz

You run terra::extract(ndvi, cbind(buffalo$lon, buffalo$lat)) and every value comes back NA. The NDVI raster is in UTM 36S. What happened?

Modern terra is helpful when it can be: give it a SpatVector or an sf object that carries a CRS, and it transforms your points for you, printing a warning:

[extract] transforming vector data to the CRS of the raster

But a plain two-column matrix has no CRS. terra can only assume the numbers are already in the raster’s coordinate system — so 31.8 and −24.5 are read as metres, land far outside the extent, and every lookup returns NA.

The same silent failure happens if the CRS of either object is missing (crs(r) <- ""). Lesson: keep your data in objects that carry their CRS, and never strip coordinates down to a bare matrix just to pass them along.

6 · Where rasters come from

Why vegetation looks the way it does

Healthy green vegetation:

  • absorbs strongly in the red (~660 nm) — chlorophyll is doing photosynthesis
  • reflects strongly in the near infrared (~850 nm) — the internal leaf structure scatters it

So the contrast between red and NIR is a measure of green biomass.

That single fact is the basis of essentially every vegetation index ever published.

Spectral signatures

Schematic curves. Vegetation is dark in the red and bright in the near infrared; soil and water are not. NDVI measures exactly that contrast.

NDVI

\[\text{NDVI} = \frac{\text{NIR} - \text{Red}}{\text{NIR} + \text{Red}}\]

  • Ranges −1 to +1. Water < 0; bare soil/rock ~0.1–0.2; sparse vegetation ~0.2–0.4; dense green vegetation 0.6–0.9
  • A proxy for green biomass and primary productivity
  • Normalised, so it partly cancels illumination and viewing-angle effects

It saturates. Above ~0.8 — closed canopy — NDVI stops responding to extra biomass. In rainforest it is nearly flat and nearly useless.

Kruger, wet and dry

MODIS NDVI at 250 m, the same landscape six months apart (dots: Cilla’s fixes). An NDVI value is only meaningful together with its date.

You cannot have all four

  • Spatial — pixel size. 10 m (Sentinel-2) … 500 m (MODIS) … 25 km (some climate products)
  • Temporal — revisit time. 5 days (Sentinel-2) vs daily (MODIS) vs 16 days (Landsat)
  • Spectral — number and width of bands. 4 broad bands vs 200 narrow (hyperspectral)
  • Radiometric — bit depth; how finely brightness is quantised

They trade off against each other: a detector collecting light from a small patch, in a narrow band, gets few photons — so it needs a longer dwell time, which means a narrower swath and a longer revisit.

Pixel size changes the landscape

The same February NDVI around Cilla’s range at MODIS resolution, then averaged to ~1 km and ~5 km. At 5 km every fix gets nearly the same value.

The missions you will actually use

Mission Spatial Temporal Since Good for
MODIS (Terra/Aqua) 250–500 m Daily 2000 Long consistent NDVI series
VIIRS 375–750 m Daily 2012 MODIS successor
Landsat 5/7/8/9 30 m 16 days 1984 Long-term landscape change
Sentinel-2 10–20 m 5 days 2015 Fine-grained habitat
Sentinel-1 (SAR) 10 m 6–12 days 2014 Cloud-free, structure, water
GEDI (LiDAR) 25 m footprint sampled 2019 Canopy height

Match the pixel to the animal and to your positional error. A 10 m Sentinel pixel is wasted on an Argos fix with 5 km error — and a 500 m MODIS pixel is useless for a dormouse.

Quiz

You study habitat selection by a forest ungulate using GPS collars (error ~15 m, hourly fixes) in a landscape with small forest patches of 1–5 ha. Which product for vegetation cover?

A 1–5 ha patch is roughly 100–220 m across. A 250 m MODIS pixel is larger than the patch, so every fix would get a mixed value averaging forest and open land — the very contrast you are trying to measure would be erased. A 1 km product is hopeless for the same reason.

Sentinel-2 at 10–20 m resolves those patches comfortably, and its 5-day revisit captures the growing season. Its resolution is also well matched to 15 m GPS error: finer would just add noise.

Sentinel-1 is excellent for structure and for cloudy regions, but it does not measure greenness — it is the right answer to a different question.
