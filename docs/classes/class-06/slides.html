Segmentation is where movement analysis stops describing and starts inferring. That is also where it gets easy to fool yourself.

1 · One track, many processes

The problem

An animal is not doing one thing all day.

  • Resting, foraging, travelling, migrating — each with its own step-length and turning-angle signature
  • Pool them and every summary is a meaningless average: a “mean step length” that describes no actual behaviour
  • Leo’s six-year track contains residency and migration. A single home range spans two continents (Class 1)

Segment first, then analyse. Almost every method in this course assumes a homogeneous process. Segmentation is how you earn that assumption.

Two questions, often confused

Change-point detection

“Where does the process change?”

Finds breakpoints in a time series. Output: a set of segments.

BCPA, segclust2d, penalised contrasts.

State classification

“Which of \(N\) recurring states is the animal in now?”

Assigns every step to a state, and states can recur.

HMM, EMbC, state-space models.

“Migration started on 14 September” is a change-point question. “Was this step foraging or travelling?” is a state question. Choose the tool that matches the question.

The families

Approach Idea Notes
Thresholding “Speed > 2 m/s = travelling” Transparent; arbitrary; no uncertainty
First-passage time Time to leave a circle of radius r Good for finding scales of area-restricted search
BCPA Change points in persistence velocity Behavioural Change Point Analysis; needs a tuning choice
Clustering / EMbC Cluster steps in (speed, turn) space No time dependence — ignores that behaviour persists
HMM Latent state chain + state-specific distributions The standard. Uses time dependence
State-space models HMM + explicit observation error Handles messy data; slower, harder

2 · Hidden Markov models

The generative story

Imagine the animal has a hidden label at each time step — say “encamped” or “travelling”.

  1. At each step the animal is in one of \(N\) states, \(S_t\)
  2. The state evolves as a Markov chain: \(P(S_t \mid S_{t-1})\) — where you go next depends only on where you are now
  3. Given the state, the observations are drawn from state-specific distributions: step length ~ gamma(\(\mu_i\), \(\sigma_i\)), turning angle ~ von Mises(\(\mu_i\), \(\kappa_i\))
  4. You never see \(S_t\). You see only the steps

The model asks: what sequence of hidden states, and what state-specific distributions, would make the observed steps most likely?

What you get out

  • State-dependent parameters — e.g. state 1: mean step 40 m, turning angles ~uniform; state 2: mean step 600 m, angles concentrated at 0
  • Transition probability matrix \(\Gamma\) — and from it the expected dwell time in each state, \(1/(1-\gamma_{ii})\)
  • The most likely state sequence (Viterbi) — one label per step
  • State probabilities per step — the honest version, with uncertainty
m <- moveHMM::fitHMM(data, nbStates = 2,
                     stepPar0 = c(50, 500, 50, 500),   # mu1,mu2,sd1,sd2
                     anglePar0 = c(0, 0, 1, 5))        # mean1,mean2,kappa1,kappa2
viterbi(m)

Why the Markov part matters

A clustering method looks at each step in isolation. An HMM knows that behaviour persists: an animal that was resting is probably still resting.

  • This borrows strength across time and produces far less “flickering” between states
  • The transition matrix is itself biology: how long does a foraging bout last?
  • Transitions can depend on covariates: is the animal more likely to start travelling when NDVI is low?
fitHMM(data, nbStates = 2, formula = ~ ndvi + cosinor(hour, period = 24))

That formula is the bridge between Class 5 and this one.

Quiz

An HMM fitted to hourly buffalo data returns two states: state 1 with mean step 45 m, state 2 with mean step 780 m. The diagonal of the transition matrix is 0.93 and 0.86. What is the expected dwell time in state 2?

For a Markov chain the expected number of consecutive steps in state \(i\) is \(1/(1-\gamma_{ii})\). Here \(1/(1-0.86) \approx 7.1\) steps, and since steps are hourly, about 7 hours.

State 1 gives \(1/(1-0.93) \approx 14\) steps, i.e. ~14 hours — plausibly the resting/ruminating state.

Note two things. First, the dwell time depends on the sampling interval: the same animal sampled every 2 hours would give a different \(\gamma_{ii}\) but a similar dwell time in hours. Second, this is a real, checkable prediction — if the model says foraging bouts last 7 hours and you know from direct observation they last 45 minutes, the model is telling you your states are not what you think.

3 · Where it goes wrong

Problem 1 — irregular sampling

HMMs as normally fitted assume a constant time step.

  • Feed them irregular data and the step-length distribution mixes intervals; the model interprets a long gap as fast movement
  • “Solutions” that are not solutions: interpolating to a regular grid invents data and biases the state distributions
  • Real solutions: resample and accept the data loss (Class 3); or use a continuous-time model (ctmm, momentuHMM with crawlWrap)

This is why the bat dataset cannot be given an HMM. 165 bursts with a median of 4 fixes cannot inform a transition matrix.

Problem 2 — how many states?

  • AIC and BIC almost always prefer more states. They are answering “which model fits best”, not “which states are real behaviours”
  • With enough data, 4 and 5 states will beat 2 and 3 every time
  • Extra states often capture outliers, or the shape of the step-length distribution, not behaviour

Choose the number of states from biology and the question, then check the fit. Pseudo-residuals and state-dependent distribution plots tell you far more than a table of AICs. Two or three states are almost always enough for what a tracking dataset can actually resolve.

Problem 3 — naming the states

The model returns state 1 and state 2. It does not return “foraging” and “travelling”.

  • Labelling them is an interpretation, and it needs external evidence: accelerometer data, direct observation, known behaviours at known times
  • “Short steps and random turns” is consistent with foraging — and with resting, with sheltering, and with the animal being dead
  • Papers that name states from step lengths alone are making an assumption, not a measurement

State the evidence for your labels, or call them “state 1” and “state 2” and describe them by their parameters.

Practical fitting advice

  • Starting values matter a lot. The likelihood is multimodal. Fit from many random starts and keep the best
  • Check for numerical zero step lengths — a gamma distribution cannot handle them. moveHMM will tell you; add a tiny constant or use a zero-inflated model
  • Always inspect pseudo-residuals (plotPR) — they are the residual plot of the HMM world
  • Plot the state-dependent densities over the histogram of your data. If they do not look like your data, nothing downstream is trustworthy

Quiz

You fit 2-, 3-, 4- and 5-state HMMs. AIC decreases every time. Which do you report?

AIC will keep improving because extra states always fit the shape of the data better — they mop up outliers and the tails of the step-length distribution. That is a statement about the flexibility of mixture models, not about the animal’s ethogram.

The defensible procedure: decide how many behavioural modes your data could plausibly resolve given the sampling interval, fit that model, and then check it — pseudo-residuals, state-dependent densities against the data, and dwell times against what you know about the species.

If a 4-state model gives you two states with nearly identical parameters, that is the model telling you it has run out of behaviour to find. Report the simpler one, and say why.

Quiz

Your 2-state HMM gives state 1 a mean step of 15 m with near-uniform turning angles. You want to call it “foraging”. What is the minimum you need?

The model returns state 1, not foraging. Short steps with unstructured turning angles are consistent with foraging — and equally with resting, ruminating, sheltering from heat, a collar that fell off, and an animal that has died. At 15 m, a good part of the apparent movement is GPS error.

To turn the label into a measurement you need something outside the trajectory: accelerometer or activity data, direct observation, known diel patterns, or a validation subset with ground-truthed behaviour.

Where you have none, the honest write-up describes states by their parameters — “a low-displacement state (mean step 15 m, dwell time ~14 h)” — and discusses the plausible behaviours without asserting one.

Recap

  • One trajectory contains several processes; segment before you analyse
  • Change-point ≠ state classification. Match the tool to the question
  • An HMM is a hidden state chain plus state-specific step/angle distributions
  • It needs regular sampling; irregular data need continuous-time methods
  • AIC will over-state the number of states. Use biology, then check residuals
  • The model gives you states. You supply the names — with evidence
