A tracking dataset on its own tells you where. Remote sensing tells you what was there — and that is what turns a map into ecology.

1 · What a sensor measures

Radiation, not “pictures”

  • The Sun emits across the electromagnetic spectrum; surfaces reflect some of it and absorb the rest
  • A passive sensor records the radiance arriving at the detector in a set of wavelength bands
  • That raw signal is contaminated by the atmosphere, the Sun’s angle, the viewing angle and the terrain
  • What you download is the result of a processing chain, and the level matters

Level 1 — top of atmosphere radiance/reflectance. Level 2 — surface reflectance, atmospherically corrected. Start here. Level 3+ — derived products: NDVI composites, land cover, LAI.

Why vegetation looks the way it does

Healthy green vegetation:

  • absorbs strongly in the red (~660 nm) — chlorophyll is doing photosynthesis
  • reflects strongly in the near infrared (~850 nm) — the internal leaf structure scatters it

So the contrast between red and NIR is a measure of green biomass.

That single fact is the basis of essentially every vegetation index ever published.

Active sensors

  • Radar (SAR) — emits microwaves and measures the return. Sees through cloud and at night. Sentinel-1. Sensitive to structure and moisture, not to greenness.
  • LiDAR — emits laser pulses, measures return time. Gives 3-D structure: canopy height, vertical complexity. GEDI (spaceborne), airborne for fine detail.

For a forest animal, canopy height and vertical structure from LiDAR are often far more informative than NDVI — greenness saturates in dense forest, but structure does not.

2 · The four resolutions

You cannot have all four

  • Spatial — pixel size. 10 m (Sentinel-2) … 500 m (MODIS) … 25 km (some climate products)
  • Temporal — revisit time. 5 days (Sentinel-2) vs daily (MODIS) vs 16 days (Landsat)
  • Spectral — number and width of bands. 4 broad bands vs 200 narrow (hyperspectral)
  • Radiometric — bit depth; how finely brightness is quantised

They trade off against each other: a detector collecting light from a small patch, in a narrow band, gets few photons — so it needs a longer dwell time, which means a narrower swath and a longer revisit.

The missions you will actually use

Mission Spatial Temporal Since Good for
MODIS (Terra/Aqua) 250–500 m Daily 2000 Long consistent NDVI series
VIIRS 375–750 m Daily 2012 MODIS successor
Landsat 5/7/8/9 30 m 16 days 1984 Long-term landscape change
Sentinel-2 10–20 m 5 days 2015 Fine-grained habitat
Sentinel-1 (SAR) 10 m 6–12 days 2014 Cloud-free, structure, water
GEDI (LiDAR) 25 m footprint sampled 2019 Canopy height

Match the pixel to the animal and to your positional error. A 10 m Sentinel pixel is wasted on an Argos fix with 5 km error — and a 500 m MODIS pixel is useless for a dormouse.

Quiz

You study habitat selection by a forest ungulate using GPS collars (error ~15 m, hourly fixes) in a landscape with small forest patches of 1–5 ha. Which product for vegetation cover?

A 1–5 ha patch is roughly 100–220 m across. A 250 m MODIS pixel is larger than the patch, so every fix would get a mixed value averaging forest and open land — the very contrast you are trying to measure would be erased. A 1 km product is hopeless for the same reason.

Sentinel-2 at 10–20 m resolves those patches comfortably, and its 5-day revisit captures the growing season. Its resolution is also well matched to 15 m GPS error: finer would just add noise.

Sentinel-1 is excellent for structure and for cloudy regions, but it does not measure greenness — it is the right answer to a different question.

3 · Spectral indices

NDVI

\[\text{NDVI} = \frac{\text{NIR} - \text{Red}}{\text{NIR} + \text{Red}}\]

  • Ranges −1 to +1. Water < 0; bare soil/rock ~0.1–0.2; sparse vegetation ~0.2–0.4; dense green vegetation 0.6–0.9
  • A proxy for green biomass and primary productivity
  • Normalised, so it partly cancels illumination and viewing-angle effects

It saturates. Above ~0.8 — closed canopy — NDVI stops responding to extra biomass. In rainforest it is nearly flat and nearly useless.

The other indices worth knowing

Index Formula Use
EVI \(2.5\frac{\text{NIR}-\text{Red}}{\text{NIR}+6\text{Red}-7.5\text{Blue}+1}\) Like NDVI but resists saturation and soil background
NDWI \(\frac{\text{Green}-\text{NIR}}{\text{Green}+\text{NIR}}\) Open water — very useful for waterholes
NDSI \(\frac{\text{Green}-\text{SWIR}}{\text{Green}+\text{SWIR}}\) Snow cover
LAI / fPAR modelled Leaf area, absorbed radiation

Beyond greenness: derived metrics

  • Rate of green-up (the derivative of NDVI in time) — this, not NDVI itself, is what drives the green wave surfing of migratory ungulates
  • Phenology metrics — start of season, peak, length
  • Terrain from a DEM: slope, aspect, ruggedness, topographic position
  • Distance to — water, roads, forest edge. Often the strongest predictor in the whole model

The best covariate is rarely the raw band. It is a transformation that corresponds to something the animal could plausibly perceive or care about.

4 · Annotation

The operation

For every fix, look up the environment there and then.

  • Static covariates — elevation, slope, distance to river. One raster; extract() and done.
  • Dynamic covariates — NDVI, temperature, snow. A stack of rasters with timestamps. You must match each fix to the right layer.

A track that spans two years annotated with a single NDVI image is not annotated — it is decorated. If your covariate varies in time and your analysis does not, you have thrown away the mechanism.

Doing it in time

# Which layer does each fix belong to?
layer_dates <- as.Date(c("2005-01-01", "2005-01-17", ...))

idx <- findInterval(as.Date(trk$t_), layer_dates)

# Extract from the whole stack, then pick the right column per row
vals <- terra::extract(ndvi_stack, vect(trk))[, -1]
trk$ndvi <- vals[cbind(seq_len(nrow(vals)), idx)]

Movebank Env-DATA does this for you, including interpolation in time, for dozens of products. Use it for real projects — but understand what it is doing first, which is what today’s exercise is for.

Scale is a decision, not a detail

  • Do you want the value at the fix, or the mean within a buffer?
  • A buffer radius is a hypothesis about perceptual range — how far the animal can assess its surroundings
  • Different radii can reverse the sign of an effect
terra::extract(ndvi, vect(trk), buffer = 500, fun = mean)

Choose the radius from biology, before you look at the results. Fitting at ten radii and reporting the best one is a garden of forking paths.

Quiz

You annotate two years of buffalo fixes with a single dry-season NDVI image and find a strong positive selection for high NDVI. What is the main problem?

The image describes one moment. Over two years, vegetation in a savanna swings enormously between wet and dry season, and the buffalo’s needs swing with it. By holding NDVI fixed you have conflated “places that were green in that dry season” with “places that are green when the animal was there”.

What you have measured is selection for persistently productive places — which may be interesting, but is not what the model claims.

The result can even be the opposite sign from the time-matched analysis: an animal may select the greenest available patch at every moment while showing no preference for places that are green on average.

Fix: use a time series and match each fix to the contemporaneous layer.

Common failures

  • CRS mismatch — or worse, passing a bare coordinate matrix (Class 2)
  • Cloud contamination — an uncomposited optical image over the tropics is mostly cloud. Use quality flags; use composites.
  • Fill values — -3000 or 32767 read as real numbers will destroy your model. Check the product’s documentation for the scale factor and no-data value.
  • Scale factors — MODIS NDVI is stored as integers; multiply by 0.0001
  • Time zones — your fixes are UTC; is the product local solar time?

After every annotation: summary() the new column. If the range is -3000 to 10000, you have raw integers, not NDVI.

Quiz

Your GPS fixes have ~15 m error. You have both a 10 m Sentinel-2 product and a 500 m MODIS product. You take the 10 m one because finer is better. When is that reasoning wrong?

Resolution should match the scale of the process, not simply be as fine as available. If an animal responds to the overall productivity of a valley, a 10 m pixel measures the individual bush it happened to stand next to — real variation, but not the variation driving the behaviour. You add noise and lose power.

The two constraints are:

  • the pixel should not be much finer than your positional error (you cannot tell which pixel the animal was in);
  • the pixel should not be much coarser than the animal’s typical displacement (Class 8 shows exactly what breaks when it is).

Between those bounds, the choice is a hypothesis about the animal’s perceptual scale — and you should state it.

Recap

  • Sensors record radiance in bands; products are processed, and the processing level matters
  • Four resolutions, all trading off. Match the pixel to the animal and to your positional error
  • NDVI is red/NIR contrast: useful, and it saturates
  • Annotation must match in space and in time; a static covariate for a dynamic process is a modelling error, not a shortcut
  • Buffer radius encodes a hypothesis about perception. Choose it first
