1 · Describing a trajectory

The step

Two consecutive fixes define a step.

  • Step length \(s_t = \lVert \mathbf{x}_{t+1} - \mathbf{x}_t \rVert\)
  • Absolute direction \(\phi_t = \operatorname{atan2}(\Delta y, \Delta x)\)
  • Turning angle \(\theta_t = \phi_t - \phi_{t-1}\), wrapped to \((-\pi, \pi]\)
  • Speed \(v_t = s_t / \Delta t\)

A turning angle needs three fixes. A step needs two. That is why filter_min_n_burst(min_n = 3) exists.

Turning angles are circular data: −179° and +179° are 2° apart, not 358°. Never take an arithmetic mean of angles.

Everything here is scale-dependent

  • Sample every 5 minutes: many short steps — but so short that GPS error randomises their direction
  • Sample hourly: clear directional persistence
  • Sample every 12 hours: few long steps, turning angles ≈ uniform (the animal looks like it moves at random)
  • Total path length grows without bound as the interval shrinks — the coastline paradox

“Mean step length” is not a property of the animal. It is a property of the animal and your sampling interval. Always report the interval alongside it, and never compare step lengths measured at different rates.

There is a sweet spot: fine enough to resolve the behaviour, coarse enough that a typical step is much longer than your GPS error.

Net squared displacement (NSD)

\[\text{NSD}(t) = \lVert \mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{x}_0 \rVert^2\]

The squared distance from the starting point. A remarkably effective diagnostic:

  • Sedentary — NSD rises then plateaus at a low value
  • Migratory — a sigmoid up, a plateau, a sigmoid back down
  • Dispersal — a single step up to a new plateau
  • Nomadic — rises roughly linearly, no plateau

Fitting these shapes as competing non-linear models (Bunnefeld et al. 2011) is a cheap and surprisingly effective way to classify movement strategies.

Quiz

Two studies report mean step length for the same population of roe deer. Study A reports 180 m, study B reports 640 m. Both are competent. What is the most likely explanation?

Step length scales with the sampling interval. An animal sampled hourly produces shorter steps than the same animal sampled every four hours, because the straight line between fixes cuts across all the wiggles in between.

This is why step length is not comparable across studies unless the interval matches, and why you must state the interval in every methods section.

It is also why Class 8’s step selection functions always compare an observed step with alternatives of exactly the same duration.

2 · Random walks as null models

Three canonical walks

Simple / BRW

Turning angles uniform; direction has no memory.

Diffusive: displacement grows as \(\sqrt{t}\).

Correlated (CRW)

Turning angles concentrated near 0; direction persists.

Locally directed, diffusive at long range.

Lévy walk

Step lengths from a heavy-tailed distribution.

Rare very long steps; super-diffusive. Much argued over.

These are null models. They tell you what a trajectory would look like if the animal responded to nothing. Departures from them are where the biology is.

Biased random walk

Add a preferred direction — towards a den, a water hole, a wintering ground — and you have a biased random walk. Combine with persistence and you get the BCRW, the workhorse null model for animals with both memory and a goal.

Class 8’s integrated step selection analysis is, in effect, a biased correlated random walk whose bias is estimated from environmental covariates. Keep that connection in mind.

3 · Home range

What is a home range?

“That area traversed by the individual in its normal activities of food gathering, mating and caring for young.” — Burt (1943)

  • Note what it excludes: exploratory forays, migration, dispersal
  • Note what it does not say: nothing about a probability distribution, nothing about a percentage
  • Powell & Mitchell (2012): a home range is a cognitive map the animal uses — and our estimators are crude proxies for it

Every estimator answers a slightly different question. “95% home range” is not a fact about the animal; it is a number produced by a method with assumptions. Say which method, and why.

MCP — minimum convex polygon

The smallest convex polygon containing \(x\)% of the fixes.

For

  • Trivial to compute and explain
  • Comparable with a century of literature
  • No smoothing parameter to argue about

Against

  • Convex — includes land the animal never used (lakes, cliffs, cities)
  • Extremely sensitive to outliers and to sample size
  • Area grows with the number of fixes; no asymptote
  • No density information — every point counts the same

Use it for comparability with old studies. Do not use it as your only estimate.

KDE — kernel density estimation

Put a small “bump” (kernel) on each fix, sum them into a utilisation distribution (UD), then draw the contour containing 95% of the volume.

  • Gives a density, not just a boundary — you can have disjoint core areas
  • The answer is dominated by the bandwidth \(h\)
    • href (reference) — assumes bivariate normal; usually over-smooths
    • hlscv (least-squares cross-validation) — often fails to converge, or fragments the UD into confetti
    • hpi (plug-in) — generally the most reliable default

Report your bandwidth selector. Two papers using “95% KDE” with different bandwidths are not comparable.

The problem KDE has with tracking data

Classical KDE assumes fixes are independent samples from the UD.

GPS fixes an hour apart are nothing of the sort — the animal was 300 m away an hour ago and will be 300 m away in an hour.

  • Effective sample size is far smaller than \(n\)
  • So the UD is under-estimated — the home range comes out too small
  • And collecting more fixes at a higher rate makes it worse, not better

This is why area estimates from the 1990s (a few dozen VHF fixes) and from today (50,000 GPS fixes) are not comparable. It is not that the animals changed.

AKDE — autocorrelated KDE

Fleming et al. (2015): first fit a continuous-time movement model to the track, estimate the autocorrelation structure, and use it to derive the effective sample size. Then do the kernel estimate.

  • Gives confidence intervals on the area — almost nothing else does
  • Handles irregular sampling gracefully
  • Tells you when your data are inadequate, instead of returning a confident-looking number
  • ctmm package; also a point-and-click web app

If you report one home-range estimate in a modern paper, make it AKDE, and report the confidence interval. If your CI spans an order of magnitude, that is the honest answer.

Quiz

You estimate a 95% KDE home range from hourly GPS data (n = 8,000) and get 12 km². A 1995 study of the same population using VHF (n = 60) reported 31 km². What is the most likely explanation?

Classical KDE assumes independent fixes. Hourly GPS locations are strongly autocorrelated, so 8,000 fixes carry the information of perhaps a few hundred independent ones — and the estimator systematically under-estimates the range. The sparse VHF fixes, days apart, were much closer to independent.

Location error does inflate areas, but it is a far smaller effect than this.

The way to settle it is AKDE: fit the movement model, get the effective sample size, and produce an area with a confidence interval. Very often the modern estimate then agrees with the old one — and you have learned that the population did not change, your method did.

Choosing an estimator

Use when Watch out for
MCP Comparing to historical literature Convexity, outliers, no asymptote
KDE Many roughly independent fixes Bandwidth choice, autocorrelation
AKDE Modern GPS data — the default Needs enough data to fit the model
LoCoH Hard boundaries (coast, cliffs) Parameter choice
dBBMM You care about the path, not the points Assumes Brownian motion

And before any of them: should you be estimating a home range at all? For Leo the vulture the answer is no — not until you have segmented the track.

Quiz

You compute a 100% MCP for an animal after 3 months, then again after 12 months. The area has tripled. What can you conclude?

The MCP is the convex hull of your points, so every new fix can only enlarge it or leave it unchanged — never shrink it. Its area therefore increases with sample size and duration without converging, which makes comparisons across different tracking durations meaningless.

This is the single biggest problem with MCP, and it is why area-vs-sample-size curves used to be standard practice.

Dropping to 95% removes the most extreme points and helps a little, but it does not fix the underlying behaviour. A genuine expansion would need to be demonstrated with an estimator that converges — AKDE, with confidence intervals, on comparable time windows.

Recap

  • Step length, turning angle and speed are scale-dependent — always report the interval
  • Turning angles are circular data
  • NSD is a cheap, powerful diagnostic of movement strategy
  • Random walks are null models; biology lives in the departures
  • MCP is comparable but crude; KDE is biased by autocorrelation; AKDE addresses it and gives you a CI
