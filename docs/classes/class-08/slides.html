1 · The problem with RSF availability

An animal cannot be anywhere

  • An RSF treats every point in the home range as equally available at every moment
  • But a buffalo at 08:00 cannot be 20 km away at 09:00. It can reach perhaps a kilometre
  • So most of the “available” set was never a real alternative
  • Availability is local, and it moves with the animal

A step selection function fixes this by defining availability from the animal’s own movement capacity: from where it was, where could it plausibly have gone?

The design

For each observed step:

  1. Take the starting point \(\mathbf{x}_{t}\)
  2. Draw \(k\) control steps from the animal’s own distributions of step length and turning angle
  3. That gives one stratum: 1 used step + \(k\) available steps, all from the same starting point at the same time
  4. Compare them with conditional logistic regression

Because the comparison is within a stratum, everything constant at that moment — time of day, season, the animal’s internal state, its identity — cancels out. That is a remarkably clean piece of control.

Conditional logistic regression

\[ P(\text{step } j \text{ chosen}) = \frac{\exp(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_j)} {\sum_{i \in \text{stratum}} \exp(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_i)} \]

  • Identical machinery to a Cox proportional hazards model — which is why survival::clogit() and amt::fit_issf() both go through coxph()
  • Each stratum contributes only the contrast between the used step and its controls
  • No intercept. Again, only relative statements
fit_issf(case_ ~ elevation_end + sl_ + log(sl_) +
                 strata(step_id_),
         data = d)

Quiz

Why does a step selection function include strata(step_id_)?

The stratum is the design. Each observed step is matched to the controls generated from the same location at the same moment, and the likelihood only ever compares within that matched set.

This is what makes availability local and time-specific — and it means anything constant within the stratum (hour of day, season, weather, the animal’s identity and internal state) is automatically controlled for, without you having to model it.

Individual-level repeated measures are a separate issue, handled by fitting per animal or by a mixed conditional model (Muff et al. 2020). Dropping strata() does not merely change the standard errors — it fits an entirely different, and wrong, model.

2 · From SSF to iSSA

The circularity problem

To generate control steps you need the animal’s step-length and turning-angle distributions. But those distributions are themselves affected by habitat — an animal moves slowly in good habitat and fast through bad.

  • Estimate them from the raw data, and they are already contaminated by selection
  • Use them to define availability, and you bake the contamination in
  • The estimated selection coefficients are then biased

Integrated step selection analysis (Avgar et al. 2016) solves this by estimating the movement kernel and the selection coefficients simultaneously, from the same model.

How iSSA does it

Include the movement variables as covariates in the conditional model:

  • sl_ and log(sl_) — together these correct the gamma step-length distribution used to generate the controls
  • cos(ta_) — corrects the von Mises turning-angle concentration
  • The fitted coefficients on these terms tell you how the realised movement kernel differs from the tentative one you sampled from
fit_issf(case_ ~ elevation_end + grassland_end +
                 sl_ + log(sl_) + cos(ta_) +
                 strata(step_id_), data = d)

Reading the movement coefficients

If controls were drawn from a gamma with shape \(k\) and scale \(\theta\):

Term Effect on the realised kernel
\(\beta_{\log(sl)}\) adjusts shape: \(k_{\text{real}} = k + \beta_{\log(sl)}\)
\(\beta_{sl}\) adjusts scale: \(1/\theta_{\text{real}} = 1/\theta - \beta_{sl}\)
\(\beta_{\cos(ta)}\) adjusts concentration: \(\kappa_{\text{real}} = \kappa + \beta_{\cos(ta)}\)

A positive \(\beta_{\cos(ta)}\) means the animal moves more directionally than your tentative distribution assumed. amt::update_gamma() and update_vonmises() do this arithmetic for you — use them rather than doing it by hand.

Interactions: where it gets interesting

The real power is asking whether movement depends on habitat:

case_ ~ grassland_end +
        sl_ + log(sl_) + cos(ta_) +
        sl_:grassland_start +        # faster in grassland?
        cos(ta_):grassland_start +   # straighter?
        strata(step_id_)
  • _start covariates modify the movement kernel — how the animal moves from that habitat
  • _end covariates capture selection — which habitat it moves into
  • Mixing them up is the single most common error in iSSA papers

Quiz

In an iSSA you find a positive coefficient on sl_:forest_start. What does it mean?

An interaction with sl_ modifies the movement kernel, not the selection term, and _start means the covariate is measured at the step’s origin. So this says: given that the animal is in forest, its steps are longer.

Ecologically that usually means the animal is transiting, not using, the forest — it crosses quickly rather than lingering.

Note how this can coexist with positive selection for forest (forest_end > 0): an animal may head into forest and then move through it rapidly. Separating “where does it go” from “how does it move once there” is exactly what iSSA buys you, and it is invisible to an RSF.

3 · Doing it well

Practical decisions

  • How many control steps? 10 is a common default; check that coefficients stabilise as you increase it, exactly as for RSF availability
  • Sampling interval. All steps in the analysis must share one duration. Resample first (Class 3)
  • Where to measure covariates. At the end point, along the step, or averaged over it? Measuring along the step is more defensible for barriers such as roads and rivers
  • Zero-length steps break the gamma distribution — amt warns and replaces them

Multiple animals, again

  • Two-stage: fit each animal, then summarise the coefficients. Simple, transparent, and adequate for small samples
  • Mixed conditional models: Muff et al. (2020) show how to fit these in glmmTMB with random slopes and a fixed large variance on the stratum intercepts
  • A random intercept alone is not enough — it does not let individuals differ in selection, which is usually the biology you care about

Validation

  • Used–habitat calibration plots (Fieberg et al. 2018)
  • Withhold whole individuals, or whole time blocks — never random steps, because autocorrelation leaks the answer into the test set
  • Simulate from the fitted model and compare the simulated space use with the observed. If your model cannot regenerate something resembling the real track, it has not captured the process
amt::simulate_path(...)   # redistribution kernel from the fitted iSSA

Quiz

You fit an iSSA with 10 control steps per stratum, then refit with 100. The coefficients barely move but all the standard errors halve. What do you report?

Stable coefficients are exactly what you want to see — it means 10 controls already characterised availability well, and that is the check worth reporting.

The shrinking standard errors are the same trap as in Class 7. Control steps describe what was available, which you can sample as finely as you like. More of them sharpen your description of availability; they do not constitute additional observations of the animal’s choices. Your real sample size is the number of steps — and, for population inference, the number of animals.

Report the effect sizes, state how many controls you used, and show that the estimates were stable with respect to that choice.

RSF or SSF?

RSF SSF / iSSA
Availability Whole domain, fixed Local, moves with the animal
Order Usually 2nd or 3rd Effectively 3rd, step-scale
Needs regular sampling No Yes
Models movement No Yes
Time-varying covariates Awkward Natural
Best for Broad-scale range placement Fine-scale decisions, corridors, barriers

If your data are regularly sampled and you care about how the animal moves through the landscape, use iSSA. If your sampling is sparse or irregular, and your question is about broad-scale range placement, an RSF is the honest choice.

The five habits worth keeping

  • Look at the data before modelling it. Plot coordinates against time. Plot step length against time gap. Every dataset in this course had a defect you could see in one plot.
  • Count what you remove, at every step, and put the table in the paper.
  • Check that an argument did what you think. buffer= was silently ignored; group_by() was silently ignored. Both cost us most of a dataset.
  • State your availability domain. Half of habitat-selection disagreements are definitional.
  • Report effect sizes, not p-values. You chose the sample size.
