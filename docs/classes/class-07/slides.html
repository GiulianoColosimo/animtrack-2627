1 · The central idea

Selection is a comparison

An animal selects a resource when it uses it disproportionately to its availability.

  • Use alone tells you nothing. A wolf in a landscape that is 95% forest will be found mostly in forest whether or not it prefers forest
  • So every selection analysis needs two samples:
    • Used — where the animal was
    • Available — where it could have been

The second sample is not data. You construct it, and the construction encodes a hypothesis. Most disagreements about habitat selection results are disagreements about availability.

Johnson’s four orders (1980)

Order Question Available =
1st Where is the species’ range? The planet
2nd Where does this individual put its home range? The study area / population range
3rd Where within its home range does it go? The home range
4th Which items does it take at a site? Items at the site

The same animal can show opposite results at different orders. A deer may place its home range in forest (2nd order) yet prefer clearings within it (3rd order). Always state your order — it is the definition of “available”.

The RSF

\[w(\mathbf{x}) = \exp(\beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \cdots + \beta_k x_k)\]

\(w(\mathbf{x})\) is the relative probability of use, proportional to the true probability by an unknown constant.

  • Fitted with logistic regression on used (1) vs available (0) points
  • There is no intercept you can interpret — it absorbs the arbitrary number of available points you drew
  • Only relative statements are licensed: “twice as likely as”, never “40% probability of use”

Used–available is not case–control

  • “Available” points are not known absences. The animal may well have used them; you just did not observe it
  • Formally, the used–available design fits an inhomogeneous Poisson point process, and logistic regression is a computational device for it
  • This is why the infinitely weighted logistic regression trick works: give available points a huge weight and the logistic coefficients converge to the correct point-process coefficients
d$w <- ifelse(d$case_, 1, 5000)
glm(case_ ~ elevation + grassland, data = d,
    family = binomial(), weights = d$w)

Quiz

Your RSF gives \(\hat\beta = 0.69\) for a standardised NDVI covariate. What can you correctly say?

RSF coefficients are on the log scale, so the effect is multiplicative: \(e^{0.69} \approx 2\). A one-standard-deviation increase in NDVI doubles the relative probability of use, holding the other covariates fixed.

“Relative” is doing essential work in that sentence. The RSF is proportional to the probability of use by an unknown constant — the intercept depends entirely on how many available points you happened to draw — so absolute probabilities are not available to you.

Standardising covariates before fitting is what makes “one SD” meaningful and lets you compare effect sizes across covariates measured in different units.

2 · Availability, the hard part

Three decisions

  1. The domain. Study area? MCP? KDE? Buffer around the track? This is your choice of Johnson order.
  2. How many points. Too few and the estimates are noisy; the coefficients should stabilise as you add more. Check that they do.
  3. How they are distributed. Uniform random is the default. But should inaccessible areas (open water, cliffs, the other side of a motorway) be “available” at all?

Changing the availability domain can reverse the sign of a coefficient. This is not a flaw in the method — it is the method working. Different domains ask different questions.

How many available points?

  • There is no magic number. The practical test: increase the number until the coefficients stop changing
  • Typically thousands to tens of thousands per animal
  • With infinite weighting, more available points is always better — the only cost is computation
for (n in c(1e3, 5e3, 2e4, 1e5)) {
  d <- random_points(trk, n = n) |> extract_covariates(covs)
  # ... refit, record coefficients, plot them against n
}

The functional response

Selection is not a fixed property of an animal.

  • An animal in a forest-rich landscape may show weak selection for forest; the same animal in a forest-poor landscape shows strong selection
  • So \(\beta\) depends on availability — the functional response in habitat use (Mysterud & Ims 1998)
  • Pooling individuals across different landscapes without modelling this averages away the very thing you are studying

Practical consequence: report the availability each individual faced, and consider modelling \(\beta\) as a function of it.

Quiz

Two studies of the same wolf population reach opposite conclusions about selection for roads. Study A used available points drawn from the whole national park; study B drew them from within each wolf’s home range. Who is right?

Study A asks a second-order question: given the whole park, do wolves put their home ranges in areas with more or fewer roads? Study B asks a third-order question: within the home range a wolf has already chosen, does it approach or avoid roads?

These routinely give opposite answers, and the pattern is common and interpretable: wolves may settle in road-poor parts of the park (avoidance at 2nd order) while using road verges and tracks as travel routes once there (selection at 3rd order).

Neither study is wrong. Both are incomplete if they do not state the order and the availability domain — which is why that belongs in the abstract, not buried in the methods.

3 · Fitting it properly

The workflow in amt

library(amt)

rsf_data <- trk |>
  filter(id == "Cilla") |>
  random_points(n = nrow(trk) * 20) |>     # available within the MCP
  extract_covariates(covs) |>
  mutate(w = ifelse(case_, 1, 5000))

fit <- glm(case_ ~ elevation + slope + grassland + trees,
           data = rsf_data, family = binomial(), weights = w)

random_points() on a track defaults to the MCP of that track — a third-order analysis. Pass an hr object to control the domain explicitly.

Standardise your covariates

rsf_data <- rsf_data |>
  mutate(across(c(elevation, slope, grassland, trees),
                \(z) as.numeric(scale(z))))
  • Makes coefficients comparable across covariates with different units
  • “Per one standard deviation” is an interpretable effect size
  • Improves numerical conditioning
  • Keep the means and SDs — you need them to back-transform for prediction

More than one animal

The wrong way: pool all fixes and fit one model. Individuals differ, and the animal with most fixes dominates.

  • Two-stage: fit each animal separately, then summarise the coefficients across individuals (mean, SE, a t-test against zero). Transparent, robust, easy to explain
  • Mixed models: random intercept and random slopes per individual. Muff et al. (2020) show the random slopes are essential — without them you under-estimate the uncertainty badly
  • A random intercept alone is not enough

For 6 buffalo, the two-stage approach is honest and adequate. For 60, use a mixed model.

Validation

\(R^2\) is not available for a point-process model. Use instead:

  • k-fold cross-validation with used–available data (Boyce et al. 2002): bin the predicted RSF values, and check that the number of withheld used points per bin increases with the bin’s rank. Report the Spearman correlation
  • Withhold whole individuals, not random points — otherwise autocorrelation leaks the answer into your test set

A model can have highly significant coefficients and no predictive ability at all. With tens of thousands of points, everything is significant. Report effect sizes and validation, not p-values.

Quiz

You fit an RSF with 25,000 available points per animal and every covariate has p < 0.001. What should you conclude?

You chose the sample size, so you chose the p-values. Doubling the available points roughly halves the standard errors without adding any information about the animal. In used–available designs, p-values are close to meaningless.

What is informative: the effect sizes (\(e^\beta\), on standardised covariates), their confidence intervals, the consistency across individuals, and out-of-sample validation.

And no — reducing the available sample to manufacture plausible p-values would be worse. It throws away precision in the estimate of availability to make a statistic look respectable. Report effect sizes.

Recap

  • Selection = use relative to availability. You construct availability
  • State your Johnson order — it is the definition of available
  • RSF coefficients are log relative effects: \(e^\beta\), and no intercept
  • Availability domain, sample size and distribution all change the answer
  • Individuals differ: two-stage, or mixed models with random slopes
  • p-values are meaningless here. Effect sizes and validation are not
